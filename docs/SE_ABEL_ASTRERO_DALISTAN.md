## INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Project Context
The journey into programming often begins with a universal experience: staring at a
screen filled with cryptic statements and feeling a mix of both frustration and fascination (Olipas,
2022). For many, this first encounter becomes a huge hurdle to jump over. Bright, capable minds
are often turned away from the world of technology, not for a lack of logical thinking, but
because the initial hurdle of mastering a programming language's strict syntax is too steep and
unforgiving. (Pellas, 2023)
But In today’s world, programming is seen as an essential skill in various fields such as
education, careers, and even daily life. However, many students find it difficult to learn coding at
the beginning. The struggle usually comes from understanding syntax, as different programming
languages have distinct rules and structures that often confuse beginners (Scaffolding Progress,
2023). Additionally, small mistakes often result in syntax errors that are difficult to debug
quickly, leading to frustration and discouragement among beginners (Palma et al., 2022).
Though there is an abundance of online programming material, the resources often suffer
from a pedagogical mismatch for novices. They are frequently structured to be too advanced or
lengthy, often due to the expertise fallacy (failing to use the clear scaffolding a beginner needs),
or they are not engaging enough, leading to passive learning and high student attrition. This is a
primary cause of 'tutorial hell,' where students consume content without gaining the practical,
problem-solving skills necessary to code. Many learners of this skill eventually feel
overwhelmed rather than motivated when faced with complex or abstract material (Teaching
Programming and CT in Early Childhood Education, 2023). Moreover, with the widespread use
of smartphones, mobile learning has become a practical method of acquiring skills, offering
accessibility, flexibility, and interactivity (Educational Mobile Apps for Programming in Python:
Review and Analysis, 2023).
This research aims to create an interactive mobile practice app that focuses on deep
comprehension and python syntax mastery, moving beyond a simple step-by-step instructional
## 2


approach. The app, Squash, will feature focused lessons and advanced practice activities. Core to
its design is a Code Corruption Model that generates syntactically incorrect code challenges for
users to debug, fostering a stronger understanding of error causes and resolution. The app will
also include an intelligent evaluation system to assess the user's written code, providing detailed
feedback on correctness and identifying specific errors. Furthermore, a levelled feature will be
incorporated to classify and track the user's programming proficiency, ensuring a continuously
challenging and engaging learning path. Through this comprehensive practice-based approach,
the project aims to offer a more effective and engaging resource for beginners, making
programming seem less daunting and more fun.

1.2 Purpose and Description
The rationale for introducing programming education at an early age extends far beyond
just entertainment or vocational preparation, it can be a foundation for future cognitive
development in various fields (Liu, 2023). In this age, a growing body of research demonstrates
that learning to code provides a suite of transferable cognitive benefits that enhance academic
performance across a wide range of disciplines. The purpose of Squash is not just to develop
early programming knowledge, leveraging the "transfer effect" of skills, one can use
programming as a modern medium to cultivate durable, higher-order thinking skills (Scherer et
al., 2021)
The primary purpose of Squash is to directly address the initial hurdle of programming
syntax mastery by leveraging mobile learning for an adaptive, practice-oriented experience. By
incorporating gamification, spaced repetition, and an adaptive difficulty model, the application
intends to serve as a supportive platform that mitigates the intimidation and frustration associated
with early coding errors, thereby accelerating the development of core programming knowledge
and higher-order thinking skills (Widowati & Tigas, 2023).
A recent study demonstrated that even limited coding practice in primary grades can
significantly improve children’s executive functions (Primary School Students’ Perceptions of
Coding and Computational Thinking Using the 3C Model, 2024). The results from this said
research was particularly striking: The cognitive gains in planning and inhibition skills achieved
after just one month of coding activities (totaling eight lessons) were equivalent to, or in some
cases greater than, the improvements observed over seven months of standard curricular
## 3


activities and spontaneous development. This finding suggests that coding is not just another
subject, but a uniquely potent tool for accelerating the development of core cognitive
architecture.

1.3 Statement of the Problem
The central problem to be addressed by the research revolves around the challenges faced
by students in acquiring programming syntax proficiency, especially among students using
mobile devices. This study attempts to answer the following research questions for the target
demographic:
- Is syntax a significant hurdle that young learners of programming encounter, and how can
a mobile-first approach to this problem be interpreted?
- To what extent can a gamified approach within a mobile application increase the
engagement and motivation of novice programming learners?
- How does using spaced repetition and instant feedback enhance syntax retention and
mastery in novice coders?
- How do micro-lessons and practice activities in an app platform impact student
motivation, persistence and confidence vs. traditional or online learning?

1.6 Scope and Delimitation
This research is focused on the design, development, and evaluation of a mobile
application for learning the fundamentals of programming. The scope of the system will
primarily cover the fundamental syntax and basic concepts of the Python programming language.
It is designed for those new learners in the programming field and is specifically for absolute
beginners with little to no prior experience. Development will center on implementing core
features like bite-sized, introductory lesson modules (the content and sequence of which are
specifically scoped to align with the foundational programming topics in the DLSUD curriculum
for 1st Year, 1st Semester Computer Science students), a Code Corruption Model for generating
syntactically incorrect code challenges, an intelligent evaluation system to assess written code
and identify specific errors, a levelled feature for tracking user proficiency, and a spaced
repetition algorithm, all developed exclusively for the Android mobile platform. The empirical
evaluation of the application's effectiveness and usability will be limited to a controlled group of
approximately 30-50 Computer Science students from De La Salle University-Dasmariñas
(DLSUD), selected based on their non-existent or minimal prior programming experience.
To ensure the project remains focused and achievable, the study is deliberately limited.
The application will not cover intermediate or advanced topics such as object-oriented
programming or data structures, in order to prevent overwhelming its target users. Furthermore,
the system is not an Integrated Development Environment (IDE) and will not support complex
project management, maintaining its purpose as a learning tool rather than a professional one.
The evaluation of the application's impact is limited to a short-term pre-test and a usability
survey, conducted over a one-week period. The study will not conduct a longitudinal analysis to
track long-term syntax retention beyond the testing phase. The initial version will only support
## 6


the Python language to ensure a high-quality learning module, and core functionalities such as
account synchronization will require an active internet connection. Finally, the machine learning
component is strictly limited to a binary classification of user proficiency (Novice or
Experienced) and does not extend to multi-level or predictive performance models beyond the
core syntax challenges.

## CHAPTER II

## REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

This chapter incorporates previous literature gathered online related to the study which
will serve as support to the development of Squash. This chapter also includes the theoretic,
conceptual framework and synthesis of this paper.

## 2.1 Local Literature
The most basic need established by the literature is the requirement for simplified design.
Sibbaluca et al. (2020) highlighted that IT barriers, such as the lack of technical skills and
complex interfaces, create limitations in digital access for new users. This finding underscores
that the initial barrier to programming, characterized by unforgiving syntax, is analogous to a
technical skill barrier, directly informing the Squash design philosophy of prioritizing
user-centric accessibility.
This challenge of complexity is further reinforced by Tuala et al. (2021), who pointed to
struggles like the complexity of devices and the necessity for patient, clear guidance for new
adopters. This justifies the choice of Squash's step-by-step, micro-lesson approach, as it aims to
provide the supportive, on-demand instruction needed to guide users through difficult syntax
rules, which is often missing in traditional mass instruction.
In contrast to these difficulties, local research strongly validates the motivational
strategies that can overcome them. Moldez et al. (2024) explored gamification in Massive Open
Online Courses (MOOCs) at the University of the Philippines Open University, demonstrating
that integrating elements like badges and progress bars significantly enhanced learner
motivation, participation, and overall satisfaction. This provides crucial evidence that
gamification is an essential component for sustaining engagement when learning a difficult
subject.
Similarly, the efficacy of mobile, gamified platforms is supported by Abenes et al.
(2023), who found that a Science-Inclusive Gamified Mobile Application (SIGMA) led to
substantial improvements in student academic performance in secondary science classes. This
result critically validates the Squash methodology as an effective tool for improving the
## 8


comprehension of complex, technical concepts like programming syntax within the local
educational context.
Collectively, these four local studies establish a clear rationale for the design of the
Squash application. The work by Sibbaluca et al. and Tuala et al. identifies the foundational need
to minimize digital complexity and provide patient guidance for novice users. Conversely, the
findings of Moldez et al. and Abenes et al. validate gamification as the key pedagogical
mechanism for enhancing motivation and achieving academic success in the learning of complex
concepts.

## 2.2 Foreign Literature
The necessity for specialized support when confronting new digital skills is a universally
observed pattern. Palma et al. (2022), in their study of technology adoption in Spain, emphasized
the crucial role of family encouragement and peer influence in motivating new users to try new
technologies. This highlights the importance of social and instructional scaffolding in fostering
digital inclusion, suggesting that the self-paced, supportive environment of a mobile app is
necessary to mitigate the initial intimidation of programming.
This intimidation is further explored by Salama et al. (2020), whose focus on the digital
divide in Egypt revealed that low digital literacy limits users’ ability to access essential online
services. Their recommendation for specialized training programs and policy interventions
supports the project's aim to provide a structured, confidence-building educational tool as a
solution for bridging the programming knowledge gap.
The complexity barrier is seen through a cultural lens by Rourke (2021), who provided a
Canadian perspective, noting that user experiences are shaped not only by access but also by
cultural attitudes and community-based support. This study underscores the importance of
designing initiatives that consider comprehensive educational opportunities and a supportive
learning context to promote technology use, directly informing Squash's need to be both globally
accessible and locally sensitive in its instruction.
This pervasive complexity, particularly in syntax, has led to research supporting
alternative pedagogical methods. Studies have consistently demonstrated the effectiveness of
error-based learning over traditional problem-solving for skill acquisition in programming.
Research by Razali et al. (2021) suggests that presenting learners with code that is syntactically
## 9


broken, followed by diagnostic and targeted feedback, significantly enhances their conceptual
understanding of error causes and resolution. This approach, which shifts the learning goal from
writing correct code to correcting incorrect code, fosters a stronger mental model of language
rules and vastly improves long-term debugging proficiency. This body of work provides the
direct pedagogical mandate for Squash's novel Code Corruption Model and its Intelligent
Evaluation System, which moves beyond simple correctness checks to provide error-specific
guidance, thereby directly addressing the high frustration and discouragement noted in other
studies.
Another crucial pedagogical mechanism to counter the passive learning phenomenon is
the implementation of Spaced Repetition. This technique, which systematically prompts the user
to revisit a concept at increasingly longer intervals, is scientifically proven to optimize long-term
memory retention by engaging active recall and fighting the forgetting curve. The effectiveness
of this method has been demonstrated in language learning platforms, such as Duolingo, where
its application in developing linguistic competence shows clear success in building durable
mastery (Peláez-Sánchez & Velásquez-Durán, 2023). Unlike these natural language applications,
Squash uniquely adapts this proven SR method to the technical challenge of programming
syntax. By integrating a proprietary Spaced Repetition Algorithm and linking it to a Random
Forest Classifier for adaptive difficulty adjustment, the Squash application ensures that syntax
concepts are not just temporarily mastered, but rather woven into the learner's long-term
programming knowledge base.
Finally, Nizam et al. (2022) examined cases in Malaysia where new users viewed
technology as both beneficial and intimidating, often feeling overwhelmed by its rapid evolution.
Their conclusion that inclusive, confidence-building initiatives are necessary to make technology
less daunting reinforces the central premise of Squash: that the application must reduce the
perception of programming as overwhelming by offering bite-sized, approachable lessons.
Collectively, these international studies establish that complexity and intimidation are
global barriers to technology adoption, mirroring the struggles of novice coders with
programming syntax. This literature provides a strong foundation for the Squash methodology by
validating the need for a simplified, confidence-building mobile platform that is culturally
sensitive and offers the supportive structure of specialized training, justifying the use of
micro-learning and an adaptive instructional model.

## 2.3 Synthesis
The design of the Squash application is a necessary, empirically supported response to the
confluence of digital accessibility challenges and validated cognitive learning methodologies.
Collectively, the review of both local and international literature establishes that complexity and
intimidation are universal barriers to technology adoption, a pattern that directly mirrors the
struggle of novice programmers with unforgiving syntax. To overcome this, the design
synthesizes three core elements: a highly simplified, mobile-first design to promote digital
inclusion; gamification, validated by local research as the key mechanism for driving motivation
and persistence against frustration; and the innovative application of Spaced Repetition
combined with a robust Random Forest classifier for adaptive and durable learning. However,
the current literature reveals a critical gap: no existing mobile solution effectively combines this
error-based learning approach via a novel Code Corruption Model with a machine learning
classification system to specifically target Python syntax debugging mastery for absolute
beginners in this localized educational context. This comprehensive, evidence-based synthesis
validates Squash as the optimal solution for creating an accessible, adaptive, and effective
pathway for programming beginners.

## 2.4 Theoretical Framework
This study is anchored on the principles of ensemble learning and the work of Almasri
and Ayesh (2020), who utilized classification algorithms, specifically Random Forest, to predict
student performance and enhance personalized learning environments. Their research
demonstrated that models like Decision Trees are effective in classifying learners based on
behavioral indicators such as quiz scores, response time, and accuracy. By analyzing these
patterns, the model can determine the learner’s proficiency level and recommend appropriate
instructional strategies. The Squash mobile educational application adopts this theoretical
foundation: that learning outcomes can be improved through adaptive systems powered by
machine learning models. The Random Forest algorithm serves as the underlying theory
supporting the app’s capacity to predict user performance and dynamically adjust learning
content according to each learner’s progress. The Random Forest model was specifically chosen
## 11


for its enhanced accuracy, robustness against overfitting, and high performance in classifying
proficiency tiers, making it a highly practical tool for delivering adaptive feedback in a real-time,
resource-constrained mobile environment. This theoretical model aligns with the idea that
personalized learning, guided by data-driven insights, can lead to improved engagement,
motivation, and retention in educational technology platforms.

## 2.5 Conceptual Framework
The conceptual framework of this study illustrates how Squash integrates machine
learning with educational design to personalize programming instruction. The process begins
with user interaction data collected from quizzes, exercises, and syntax challenges. This data
serves as input for the Random Forest model, which classifies the learner into one of two
definitive proficiency tiers: Novice or Experienced. This Performance Data is a composite metric
based on user actions such as quiz scores, response time, accuracy, and the number of hints used.
Based on this precise binary classification, the app tailors the subsequent lessons and exercises to
match the learner’s skill level. For example, a Novice learner receives simpler syntax activities
with guided hints, while an Experienced learner is given more complex challenges to reinforce
mastery. Feedback from user performance continuously updates the model, creating a feedback
loop that refines learning paths over time.
Conceptually, this framework highlights a cyclical adaptive learning process:
● User Interaction -> generates performance data (Composite Metric).
● Random Forest Model -> analyzes and classifies learner proficiency into Novice
or Experienced.
● Adaptive Content Delivery -> customizes lessons and exercises (using Spaced
## Repetition).
● Feedback Loop -> updates learner profile and refines predictions.

## CHAPTER III

## METHODOLOGY

This chapter systematically details the plan and processes undertaken to achieve the
research objectives of developing and evaluating the Squash mobile educational application. It is
structured into three main sections: the overall research design, the development methodology
utilized for the application's construction, and the empirical evaluation strategy, which includes
the tools and procedures for assessing the app's effectiveness and usability among the target
group of novice programmers.

## 3.1 Research Design
The research employed a Design and Development (D&D) methodology to
systematically create and evaluate the Squash mobile educational application. This approach
involves two primary stages: a rigorous development phase and an empirical evaluation phase.
The development of the application was guided by the Agile software development
methodology, allowing for iterative and flexible feature implementation to align with best
practices and stakeholder feedback. The empirical evaluation phase utilized a quantitative
research design, specifically a pre-test/post-test approach combined with a
descriptive-quantitative analysis of a usability survey, to measure the application's effectiveness
on user learning outcomes and its overall usability.

## 14


## 3.2 Overall System Design
The Squash system is founded on a three-tiered architectural design model comprising
the Mobile Client, the Back-end Server, and the Database. The Mobile Client, built for the
Android platform, serves as the primary user interface where learners interact with lessons,
exercises, and the Code Corruption Model challenges. This client streams Performance Data to
the Back-end Server, which is the central hub for all core logic. The server hosts two major
machine learning components: the Random Forest Classifier for adaptive difficulty and the
pre-tuned T5 Model for intelligent feedback generation. The server executes the Random Forest
Classifier, the T5 Model, and the Spaced Repetition Algorithm, connecting to the Database for
permanent storage of user profiles and learning progress.

3.2.1 System and Software Specifications
The mobile application will be natively developed for the Android ecosystem
using the Flutter framework and the Dart programming language. The application content
focuses on the Python programming language. The Back-end Server logic is complex,
requiring specific tools for its dual machine learning functions. The Random Forest
Classifier is implemented using Python and a standard library (e.g., scikit-learn). For the
intelligent feedback system, a pre-tuned T5 Model is utilized for its natural language
generation capabilities. This model will be integrated via a suitable framework (e.g., a
Hugging Face implementation or equivalent deep learning framework) to power the
high-quality, explanatory error feedback. All persistent data will be managed using a
secure NoSQL database solution.

## 15


## 3.2.2 Machine Learning Models
The system's adaptive core relies on two distinct machine learning models:
- Random Forest Classifier (RF): This ensemble algorithm is used for adaptive
classification, predicting a learner's proficiency based on the Composite Metric
(quiz scores, response time, accuracy, and hints used). Its output is the binary
classification: Novice or Experienced, which drives the Spaced Repetition
schedule and lesson difficulty.
- Pre-tuned T5 Model: This Transformer-based model is utilized for the Intelligent
Evaluation System to provide natural language generation. Its role is not
classification but rather to take a syntactically incorrect code snippet and the
identified error from the Code Corruption Model and generate a human-readable,
specific, and pedagogical explanation or correction hint for the user. This ensures
the feedback is not just an error code but a personalized learning moment.

## 3.2.3 Implementation
Implementation will follow an iterative Agile methodology, organized into sprints
focused on the core innovative features. The initial phase establishes the full-stack
architecture. Subsequent phases involve the detailed coding of the Code Corruption
Model, which generates the syntax challenges. Concurrently, the back-end will focus on
two major parallel implementations: the integration and training of the Random Forest
Classifier for the adaptive leveling, and the fine-tuning and integration of the pre-tuned
T5 Model to power the Intelligent Evaluation System's generation of targeted,
## 16


instructional error feedback. The final step is testing the full adaptive cycle with the
Spaced Repetition Algorithm linked to the Random Forest's output.

3.3 Respondents of the Study
The respondents for the empirical evaluation of the Squash application will consist of
approximately 30 to 50 Computer Science students enrolled in the 1st Year, 1st Semester
curriculum at De La Salle University-Dasmariñas (DLSUD). A Purposive Sampling technique
will be employed to ensure that the selected participants meet the study's precise scope: they
must be absolute beginners with non-existent or minimal prior programming experience, thereby
ensuring the application is evaluated on its intended target demographic. This sample size is
deemed appropriate for a short-term, controlled usability and effectiveness study, as detailed in
the scope and delimitation.

## 3.4 Data Gathering Procedure
The data gathering will proceed in a defined sequence: First, formal approval from the
DLSUD institution and informed consent from all participants will be secured. Second, a Pre-test
will be administered to establish a baseline of the respondents' current Python syntax knowledge.
Third is the Intervention Phase, where all participants will be instructed to use the Squash mobile
application for a controlled period of one week, completing a standardized set of lessons and
exercises. Finally, the Post-Intervention Data Collection will involve two components: the
administration of a usability survey to assess the user experience, and the automated system data
extraction of the Composite Metric (performance data) from the server logs for all participants.

## 17


## 3.5 Data Analysis
The collected data will be subjected to appropriate statistical analyses to test the
hypotheses and evaluate the application's performance. To determine the effectiveness of the
Squash application on learning outcomes, the results of the pre-test and post-test will be
compared using a Paired Samples t-test. The data from the usability survey, which employs a
Likert scale, will be analyzed using Descriptive Statistics (e.g., mean, standard deviation,
frequency distribution) to summarize user perceptions of the app’s engagement and ease of use.
Additionally, the performance data extracted from the system will be analyzed to calculate the
accuracy and efficiency metrics of the Random Forest Classifier to validate the operational
success of the machine learning component.
